When should you go to sleep?

You sleep in cycles of about 90 minutes. Wake up at the end of one and you feel fine; wake up in the middle of one and you feel wrecked. Pick a time and this works out the rest.

Why 90 minutes?

A cycle isn't one kind of sleep

Each cycle moves from light sleep, down into deep slow-wave sleep, then up into REM, where most dreaming happens. Then it starts over. One lap takes roughly 90 minutes.

Grogginess is about timing, not just hours

An alarm that goes off during deep sleep drags you out of the heaviest stage there is — that thick, confused feeling is called sleep inertia. Landing at the end of a cycle, when you're already in light sleep, is a much softer exit.

Aim for five or six cycles

That's 7.5 to 9 hours, which is what most adults actually need. Four cycles is a short night you can survive; three is a rescue plan, not a routine.

Treat it as a guide

Real cycles run anywhere from about 70 to 120 minutes, they stretch and shift through the night, and no one falls asleep on command. This gets you close — it isn't a stopwatch.

Things that actually help

  • Keep the same schedule. A steady wake-up time does more than any single early night.
  • Cut caffeine by early afternoon. Half of it is still in you six hours later.
  • Dim the lights an hour before bed. Bright light tells your body it's still daytime.
  • Keep the room cool and dark. Body temperature has to drop for you to fall asleep.
  • If you're awake after 20 minutes, get up. Do something dull in low light, then try again.
